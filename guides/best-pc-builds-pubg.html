Best PC Builds for PUBG in 2026: Budget, Mid-Range & High-End

PUBG PC Guide · Updated November 2026 · 7 min read

PUBG rewards smooth frame rates more than pretty graphics: a steady 100-plus FPS makes aiming, tracking, and reacting noticeably easier. All three builds are balanced so no single part holds the rest back.

What PUBG Actually Needs From a PC

PUBG is a large open-world shooter that leans on both the processor and the graphics card. The CPU handles the huge map, dozens of players, vehicles, and physics, while the GPU draws the detailed terrain and long sightlines. That is why a balanced build beats a lopsided one: a top-end graphics card with a weak processor will still stutter in busy areas. PUBG uses a lot of RAM: 16 GB is now the realistic minimum, and 32 GB gives you comfortable headroom for Discord, a browser, and background apps. Finally, an SSD is essential: PUBG loads slowly from a hard drive, and texture pop-in can leave you staring at blurry buildings when you land.

Budget Build: Smooth 1080p for Around $700

The budget build targets a steady 80 to 100 FPS at 1080p on competitive settings: medium textures, low shadows and effects. A six-core Ryzen 5 5600 or Intel Core i5-12400F gives PUBG plenty of power, paired with an RTX 4060 or RX 7600 class card. Add 16 GB of DDR4 in two sticks for dual channel, plus a 1 TB NVMe SSD. A reliable 550-watt power supply from a known brand is enough. Skip RGB lighting and a fancy case at this level: every dollar is better spent on the graphics card and SSD, the parts you will actually feel in the game.

Mid-Range Build: 144 FPS at 1080p or 1440p for Around $1,200

The mid-range build is the sweet spot for most players, targeting 144 FPS to match a 144 Hz monitor and make the game feel far more responsive than 60 FPS. A modern eight-core Ryzen 7 7700 or Intel Core i5-14600K handles PUBG's busy scenes easily, and an RTX 4070 Super or RX 7800 XT class card delivers high frame rates even at 1440p. Go with 32 GB of DDR5 here, which leaves room for streaming or recording. A 1 TB NVMe SSD and a 750-watt power supply leave headroom for future upgrades. A decent air cooler is worth the small extra cost: it keeps the CPU quiet and cool during long sessions.

High-End Build: Maximum Performance for Around $2,000+

The high-end build is for players who want the best: 200-plus FPS at 1440p, or smooth 4K play, plus power for streaming and recording at the same time. A Ryzen 7 9800X3D is a standout for PUBG: its large cache helps exactly the simulation-heavy work this game does, and it delivers some of the highest frame rates in the game. Pair it with an RTX 5070 Ti or RX 9070 XT class card, 32 GB of fast DDR5, and a 2 TB NVMe SSD. An 850-watt power supply and a case with good airflow complete the build. The gains over the mid-range build are real but smaller than the jump from budget to mid-range, so only spend this much if you want the best or also play demanding games beyond PUBG.

Parts You Should Not Cheap Out On

Three parts are worth buying from trusted brands even on a tight budget: the power supply, the SSD, and the case airflow. A cheap power supply can damage your whole PC, so look for an 80 Plus Bronze rating or better. A slow SSD makes the whole system feel sluggish, so stick to NVMe drives from known brands. A case with a solid front panel and no intake fans will cook your parts; a mesh front with two or three fans costs little and keeps temperatures far lower. The monitor matters too: a PC that pushes 144 FPS needs a 144 Hz screen to show them, so budget for one with the mid-range and high-end builds.

Before you buy: prices change fast, and a card that is good value today can be a bad deal next month. Check prices for at least two options in each tier, buy the CPU and motherboard as a matching pair, and confirm the parts fit your case before ordering.

Prebuilt or Build It Yourself?

Building your own PC usually saves money and lets you pick every part, and modern parts are easy to assemble: mostly screws and labelled plugs, with excellent step-by-step video guides available. A prebuilt is still a good choice if you want a warranty on the whole system and no assembly hassle. If you buy prebuilt, check the exact parts inside rather than the marketing name: some sellers pair a strong graphics card with slow single-stick memory or a weak power supply. Either way, use the builds above as a shopping checklist: if a prebuilt matches one of these tiers at a fair price, it will run PUBG well.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much should I spend on a PC for PUBG?

Around $700 gets you smooth 1080p play, and around $1,200 is the sweet spot for 144 FPS on a high refresh rate monitor. Spending more than $2,000 only makes sense if you want maximum settings, 4K, or streaming alongside the game.

Is 16 GB of RAM enough for PUBG in 2026?

Yes, 16 GB is enough to run PUBG smoothly on its own. Choose 32 GB if you keep Discord, a browser, or streaming software open while you play, or if you are building on a current DDR5 platform where 32 GB is now the sensible standard.

Do I need a 144 Hz monitor for PUBG?

You do not need one, but it makes a real difference once your PC can push more than 60 FPS. A 144 Hz monitor shows the extra frames your PC produces, making movement and aiming feel smoother. Pair a 144 Hz screen with the mid-range build for the best value.
